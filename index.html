Abstract

One morning in October I woke up convinced that I would be the next great quant, and that the quants in Sydney were about to watch their strategies lose their alpha once I was finished with these markets. I had studied financial markets at university, I knew enough Python to write a backtest, and I had a fresh week of Claude usage with which to build the next Optiver. The plan followed from one of the first things a finance student learns, that markets reward investors who buy and hold, and since a prop firm effectively lends a trader leverage on a small deposit, buying and holding through a prop-firm evaluation looked like a reasonable way to collect the payout.

A prop-firm evaluation works as follows: the trader pays a fee, $80 in Vest Capital's case, for an account holding $10,000 of simulated money, and if the account grows by 10% before it loses 6%, the firm funds it and pays the trader 80% of the profit it makes from then on. Passing it needed more than buying and holding, so I backtested thousands of variations of the strategies popular with trading educators, from opening-range breakouts to fair value gaps and inverse fair value gaps, and none of them held up once costs were included. That outcome is what my lectures on market efficiency would have predicted, since in liquid markets an edge from market microstructure requires an information or technology advantage that a student working from a laptop does not have, and so I stopped trying to beat the market and started looking at the evaluation itself.

Looked at closely, the evaluation is less a test of skill than a contract with a fixed price and two outcomes, a profit target that pays and a loss limit that does not, which makes it an option that can be valued like any other. To value it, suppose my pet goldfish were given a trading account and made every decision with exactly zero edge: its account would reach the target before the loss limit 37.5% of the time, and once the funded account it might win is valued, the goldfish's $80 fee buys about $180 of expected payout. Vest's pricing is not a mistake, because the fee is set for customers who do worse than the goldfish, typically by over-trading and paying costs on every trade, but it does mean that a buyer who trades mechanically, as the goldfish would, holds a contract worth more than it cost.

Even at those odds the goldfish still loses its $80 in most evaluations, so the strategy hedges each account with real money on Hyperliquid, where the Nasdaq 100 also trades as a perpetual future. Because most of the $82,000 position on Vest is simulated money, the hedge only needs to cover the part that can turn into real cash, which is about 30 cents per dollar of position during the evaluation and 80 cents once the account is funded. At those sizes each account pays roughly the same whether it passes or fails, which turns what was a lottery ticket into something much closer to a steady return.

Vest is new, so only about three months of prices are available, but once every fee, spread, delay and trading rule is charged, about half of the theoretical value survives, which comes to roughly $50 per account and about 11 percent a month on the capital tied up, until Vest's limit of ten funded accounts caps the strategy near $24,000 a month. That result holds through the sensitivity analysis and stress tests and turns negative only if hedge orders slip badly or arrive too late. Since 29 October, when the design was frozen, the strategy has also been running forward on prices recorded after that date as an out-of-sample test, shown below and updated daily.

Whether the result survives real orders depends on execution, so the next step, now under construction, is a Python engine that sends the Vest order and the Hyperliquid hedge at the same moment, keeps the delay between them as short as possible and handles the edge cases, such as one leg filling without the other or an exchange disconnecting mid-trade. The quants in Sydney can keep their alpha for now, because I set out to build the next Optiver and ended up with a very small hedge fund instead, one whose edge comes not from predicting the market but from noticing that a prop firm had sold me an option for less than it was worth.

1. Executive Summary

This paper answers a question most traders never ask about a prop-firm evaluation, which is what it is worth, not how to pass it. Priced as the double-barrier digital option it really is, Vest Capital's $10,000 evaluation is worth about $180 to a trader with no skill against an $80 fee, and a hedge on Hyperliquid sized to the account's real exposure turns that value into a steady return: about $50 per account and 11 percent a month on capital after every measured cost, a result that has held on prices recorded since the strategy was fixed. Figure 1 shows how the hedge turns the two possible outcomes of the evaluation into the same result.

The case rests on seven findings, each answered in its own section:

  1. The evaluation is mispriced. A coin flip passes it 37.5 percent of the time, while the $80 fee breaks even only at a 22 percent pass rate, and 52 of the 57 evaluations on sale carry the same gap (Section 2).
  2. The mispricing is rational for Vest. The price is set for customers who trade worse than a coin flip, and against them it works; the gap belongs only to a buyer who removes the behaviour the price relies on (Section 3).
  3. The hedge follows delta, not notional. Sized at 0.30 of the position during the evaluation and 0.80 once funded, it makes the real payoff the same whether the account passes or fails, while a one-for-one hedge adds risk (Section 4).
  4. Execution decides half the value. Vest's daily loss limit and the choice of order type matter more than fees, and post-only limit entries are worth $77 per account against $37 with market orders (Section 6).
  5. Capital, not account size, sets the return. Small evaluations traded with large positions earn about 11 percent a month on capital up to $150,000, before Vest's ten-account limit caps the strategy near $24,000 a month (Section 7).
  6. The result holds out of sample. It was profitable in every period tested, including a holdout recorded after the design was fixed and a forward test that updates daily, and only failures in hedge execution turn it negative (Section 8).
  7. It is built to run. The data, the research engine and a trading platform for Vest were built for this work, largely by directing AI coding agents against test suites, and a Python engine that sends both legs of each trade at the same moment is being built now (Section 9).

The recommendation is to proceed in stages, testing live hedge execution with a single account before committing further capital, because hedge execution is the one factor that can turn the result negative and it is also the cheapest to measure (Section 10). Finding a mispriced opportunity and managing its risk is what hedge funds do; this one simply happens to be very small, run by a student, and capped by the firm that sells the product.

What This Project Demonstrates

  • Commercial judgement, for consulting: recognising that a product is priced on customer behaviour, explaining why that price is still rational for the seller, and setting out what the seller should change (Sections 2, 3 and 10.3).
  • Derivatives and hedging, for sales and trading: pricing the contract as a barrier option and hedging its delta instead of its notional, with basis and liquidation risk managed (Sections 2 and 4).
  • Execution and market microstructure, for sales and trading: order types and their fees, stop rules tested on minute data, time-of-day effects and intraday sizing (Section 6).
  • Capital allocation and risk, for a hedge fund: return on capital, Kelly sizing, portfolio construction and an honest capacity ceiling (Section 7).
  • Research rigour, for a quant firm: pre-registered studies, paired tests, a holdout, a daily forward test and formal checks for overfitting (Sections 5 and 8).
  • Building with AI, for BCG X: a data recorder, a research engine and a trading platform, each built because the analysis needed it, with low-latency two-leg execution under construction (Section 9).

It also demonstrates an unusually high tolerance for reading prop-firm terms and conditions, which is rarer than any of the above.

Methods and Structure

The valuation uses first-passage results for Brownian motion (the gambler's-ruin problem), the discrete-monitoring correction of Broadie, Glasserman and Kou (1997) and the drawdown result of Lehoczky (1977). Capital allocation follows Kelly (1956) and Markowitz (1952), with only real wealth counted as the bankroll. Execution modelling follows Almgren and Chriss (2001), and the comparison across exchanges allows for non-synchronous trading as in Lo and MacKinlay (1990). Selection bias is checked with Bailey and López de Prado (2014), Bailey et al. (2017) and Benjamini and Hochberg (1995). To my knowledge, this is the first work to value retail prop-firm evaluations or to hedge them across exchanges.

Throughout, I assume claims are paid at the documented share, which matches Vest's published terms and my own funded accounts. The paper follows the questions an investor would ask in order: whether the evaluation is mispriced (Section 2), why the mispricing persists (Section 3), how the risk is hedged (Section 4), how the strategy is tested (Section 5), what execution costs (Section 6), how far the strategy scales (Section 7), whether it holds out of sample (Section 8), how it was built (Section 9), and what remains at risk and what to do next (Section 10), before concluding in Section 11.

2. Is the Evaluation Mispriced?

Valued as a contract, the $10,000 evaluation is worth about $180 to a trader with no skill, more than twice its $80 fee. Although the evaluation is sold as a test of trading ability, its terms describe something an options desk would recognise at once, namely a payout that depends only on which of two levels the account reaches first, and because a payout of that kind can be priced exactly, this section sets out the terms, prices the two stages of the account in turn and shows that the same gap runs across almost all of Vest's range.

Each account's name encodes these terms, so that "1-Step 10k 10% DLL3 80%" describes a one-step evaluation on a $10,000 balance with a 10% profit target, a 3% daily loss limit and an 80% profit share. This account serves as the running example throughout the paper, with a fee of \(F = \$80\), a loss limit of \(D = \$600\), a profit target of \(G = \$1{,}000\) and a profit share of \(s = 0.80\).

2.2 How Does an Account Progress?

An account passes through two stages whose payoffs differ in kind, and that difference shapes both the valuation and the hedge. The evaluation stage ends at one of two limits, with the account either passing at \(+G\) and becoming funded or failing at \(-D\) and closing, so its payoff is all-or-nothing, whereas the funded stage that follows pays \(s\) of any profit withdrawn as a stream of cash until the balance falls \(D\) below its starting level and the account closes for good. Because the two stages pay out so differently, the hedge ratio changes at the moment of funding (Section 4), and Figure 2 traces the full lifecycle.

2.3 Where Does Real Money Change Hands?

Since every balance inside the account is simulated, real money enters or leaves the strategy through only three channels, namely the fee paid to Vest, the payouts received from Vest and the profit or loss on the Hyperliquid hedge:

\[ \Pi = \Pi_{\text{hedge}} + C - F - c_{\text{hedge}} - f_{\text{hedge}} \tag{1} \]

where \(\Pi_{\text{hedge}}\) is the profit on the Hyperliquid position, \(C\) is cash received from claims, \(F\) is the fee, and \(c_{\text{hedge}}\) and \(f_{\text{hedge}}\) are Hyperliquid trading fees and funding. Keeping simulated and real amounts in separate ledgers makes it possible to measure deployed capital, the quantity the strategy is optimised against, as the fee plus the Hyperliquid margin plus any hedge losses carried before payouts arrive, taken at the 95th percentile across simulated account lives. Figure 3 shows how the two accounts connect to the investor's capital.

2.4 Why Is the Evaluation an Option?

An evaluation behaves like leverage with a capped downside, which is the key to valuing it. In the running example the position is \(N \approx \$82{,}000\), about 8.2 times the displayed balance and roughly a thousand times the fee, yet the buyer's real loss can never exceed the fee \(F\) and the real gain can never exceed the value of a funded account, so the evaluation's worth depends on only two quantities, the probability of passing and the value of the funded account that a pass delivers. In option terms, the evaluation stage is a double-barrier digital option, paying a fixed amount, the value of a funded account, if simulated profit reaches the target before the loss limit and expiring worthless otherwise, with the fee as its premium, while the funded stage is a claim on a share of profit that ends at a fixed drawdown. Sections 2.5 and 2.6 price the two parts in turn.

2.5 How Likely Is a Pass?

I value the account for a trader with no forecasting ability, because that is the edge available to any buyer without having to assume skill. Under that assumption the account's simulated profit behaves like a random walk with no trend, in which each move is as likely to be up as down, starting at zero and ending as soon as it reaches either \(+G\) or \(-D\). This is the classic gambler's-ruin problem, and the probability of reaching the target first is

\[ P(\text{pass}) = \frac{D}{G + D} \tag{2} \]

where \(D\) is the loss limit and \(G\) is the profit target. For the running example,

\[ P(\text{pass}) = \frac{600}{1{,}000 + 600} = 0.375. \]

Position size, notably, does not appear in equation (2), which means that a larger position leaves the chance of passing unchanged and affects only how long the evaluation is expected to last,

\[ \mathbb{E}[\tau] = \frac{G\,D}{\sigma^{2}}, \tag{3} \]

where \(\sigma\) is the dollar volatility of the position, which rises in proportion to its notional \(N\). Doubling the position therefore cuts the expected duration by a factor of four without changing the chance of passing, and this matters for the strategy because an account that resolves faster frees its capital sooner. Figure 4 simulates the process.

2.6 What Is a Funded Account Worth?

A funded account has no profit target and closes only when its balance falls \(D\) below its starting level. Because profit is withdrawn as soon as it accrues, the balance never rises above its start, so the fixed loss limit works in practice as a drawdown of \(D\) from the highest point of cumulative profit, and for a trader with no skill the expected profit withdrawn before that drawdown occurs equals \(D\) (Lehoczky, 1977). The cash value of a funded account is therefore

\[ V_{\text{funded}} = s\,D, \tag{4} \]

where \(s\) is the profit share. For the running example, \(V_{\text{funded}} = 0.80 \times 600 = \$480\).

2.7 What Is the Evaluation Worth?

Combining the probability of passing in equation (2) with the funded value in equation (4) gives the expected value of the evaluation, net of the fee,

\[ \begin{aligned} V &= P(\text{pass})\,V_{\text{funded}} - F \\ &= \frac{s\,D^{2}}{G + D} - F. \end{aligned} \tag{5} \]

For the running example,

\[ V = 0.375 \times \$480 - \$80 = \$180 - \$80 = \$100. \]

Figure 5 sets out the same calculation as a probability tree, with each branch showing the chance of an outcome and the real payoff it delivers.

On average, then, the evaluation returns $180 for an $80 fee, a gap of $100 per account before any costs are counted. Multi-step evaluations follow the same logic with every step required to pass, and Appendix A gives their general form.

The gap is not confined to one account but runs across almost the whole of Vest's range, as Figure 6 shows by applying equation (5) to all 57 evaluations on sale.

3. Why Does the Mispricing Persist?

Vest's price is set for the customers it actually has, and against them it works. The fee breaks even for a trader who passes about 22 percent of the time, a bar that is generous to a coin flip but not to the typical customer, who over-trades and pays costs on every impulse, so Vest earns on the majority in much the same way that a gym earns on members who stop turning up (DellaVigna and Malmendier, 2006), and the opportunity belongs only to a buyer who removes the behaviour the price relies on.

3.1 Who Is the Price Set For?

The pricing remains rational for Vest because it reflects how its customers actually trade, not what the contract is worth. The fee breaks even against customers with a small negative edge, which is realistic for retail traders once spreads, fees and over-trading are counted, and many of those customers buy a new evaluation after failing, so each one tends to contribute several fees before a payout is ever made. The gap is therefore open only to a buyer who avoids those losses, trades at very low cost and hedges what remains, and Section 6 measures how much of it survives the daily loss limit and the transaction costs that equation (5) leaves out.

Industry figures support this reading. Retail prop firms earn most of their revenue from evaluation fees, and reported pass rates sit between roughly 5 and 14 percent depending on the firm and the challenge (Track360, 2026), well below the 22 percent at which Vest's fee breaks even, which means the typical customer funds the payouts of the few who pass and leaves Vest's pricing comfortably safe against the customers it expects. Because those figures come from industry trackers whose methods are not fully disclosed, they indicate an order of magnitude and no more.

3.2 At What Skill Does the Fee Break Even?

Since the value of normal trading depends heavily on skill, the clearest way to see who the fee is set for is to measure skill by the probability of passing. A trader with no skill passes 37.5% of the time, a better trader passes more often and a trader who over-trades or pays high costs passes less often, and Table 2 values the same $10,000 evaluation, traded normally without a hedge, for five such types of trader.

Even where its expected value is positive, however, normal trading loses on most outcomes, since the trader with no skill expects +$100 per account but still loses the fee 62.5% of the time, and it is this lopsided payoff that the hedge in Section 4 is designed to remove.

3.3 What Could Vest Change?

The cleanest fix for Vest would be price. At about $180, the expected payout to a trader with no skill, the $10,000 evaluation would be worth nothing to a mechanical buyer, while a trailing loss limit or a time limit during the evaluation would close part of the gap by lowering the pass rate. Either change would leave Vest's economics against its typical customer intact, since those customers already pass well below the 22 percent break-even rate, which is why the gap is better read as a deliberate pricing choice with a narrow blind spot than as an error.

4. How Is the Risk Hedged?

The hedge is a short position on Hyperliquid's Nasdaq 100 contract, sized at 0.30 of the Vest position during the evaluation and 0.80 once the account is funded, which makes the real payoff the same whether the account passes or fails. The hedge is far smaller than the position because most of the $82,000 on Vest is simulated money and only the part that can turn into real cash needs protecting, in the same way that a bank hedges a mortgage book with interest rate swaps sized to the book's sensitivity to rates and not to its face value.

The need for a hedge comes from the lopsided payoff in equation (5), where the fee is lost with probability 0.625, and the hedge addresses it by holding an opposite position on Hyperliquid of \(h\) dollars for each dollar of evaluation position.

4.1 Why Hyperliquid?

Hyperliquid's Nasdaq 100 contract tracks Vest's closely enough to serve as the hedge. The evaluation trades the Vest Nasdaq 100 perpetual (NDX-USD-PERP) while the hedge uses the Nasdaq 100 perpetual on Hyperliquid (xyz:XYZ100), a contract listed by a third party, and because hourly returns on the two have a correlation of 0.991, an equal and opposite position removes 98.2% of the price risk. The two contracts do follow slightly different index prices, with Hyperliquid trading on average about 0.6% below Vest and the gap drifting slowly between 0.25% and 1.0%, which is why positions and stops are set in percentage terms and not as price levels. At one-minute frequency, and after adjusting for non-synchronous trading, neither exchange leads the other, while Hyperliquid funding averaged 0.05 basis points per hour, with longs paying shorts 89% of the time.

4.2 How Large Should the Hedge Be?

How large the hedge should be depends on a question that is easy to overlook, namely how much of the money in the account is real. The evaluation holds an $82,000 position, yet the buyer's real outcome ranges only from losing the $80 fee, if the account fails, to receiving a funded account worth about $480, if it passes, so across the $1,600 of simulated profit that separates the two limits the account's real value changes by just $480. In general terms, with simulated profit moving by \(G + D\) between the limits while real value moves from zero to \(V_{\text{funded}}\), the hedge ratio is

\[ h = \frac{V_{\text{funded}}}{G + D} = \frac{s\,D}{G + D}. \tag{6} \]

For the running example,

\[ h = \frac{480}{1{,}000 + 600} = 0.30. \]

The result, around 30 cents of real value for every simulated dollar, is what the hedge needs to offset, which is why the position on Hyperliquid is set at 0.30 of the Vest position during the evaluation and not at the full $82,000. Figure 9 shows the same relationship as a straight line from the loss limit to the target, with a slope of 0.30.

The ratio rises once the account is funded, because from that point Vest pays out 80% of any profit withdrawn and each simulated dollar is worth 80 cents in cash, so the hedge is increased to \(h_{\text{funded}} = s = 0.80\) at the moment the account passes. Taken together, these two ratios measure the account's real-money delta in each stage, which is a quite different quantity from the ratio of about 1.02 that best matches the price movements of the two contracts: that ratio would hedge the price of the Nasdaq, whereas the strategy needs to hedge the cash the investor actually stands to gain or lose.

4.3 What Does the Hedge Change?

The clearest way to see what the hedge does is to write down the real payoff in each outcome. With hedge ratio \(h\), the real payoffs when the evaluation fails and when it passes are

\[ \begin{aligned} \Pi_{\text{fail}} &= h\,D - F, \\ \Pi_{\text{pass}} &= V_{\text{funded}} - h\,G - F. \end{aligned} \tag{7} \]

Setting the two payoffs equal recovers equation (6), and for the running example at \(h = 0.30\) both come to the same figure,

\[ \begin{aligned} \Pi_{\text{fail}} &= 0.30 \times 600 - 80 = \$100, \\ \Pi_{\text{pass}} &= 480 - 0.30 \times 1{,}000 - 80 = \$100. \end{aligned} \]

The same holds once the funded stage is included, as Figure 10 shows by following every complete path of the account to the same real payoff.

The effect of the hedge is to narrow the range of outcomes while leaving the average unchanged. Without it, the account loses the $80 fee in 62.5% of cases and returns about $400 in the remainder, an average of $100 with a standard deviation of $232 that is realised only through a small number of large wins. With the hedge at 0.30, the Hyperliquid position gains around $180 when the account fails and loses around $300 when it passes, so both outcomes settle at the same $100 and the standard deviation falls to zero. The average does not move because a hedge on a market with no trend earns nothing over time, which limits its contribution to removing the uncertainty around the expected value, and Figure 11 shows both payoffs across the full range of hedge sizes.

Hedging one-for-one, by contrast, overshoots and replaces one gamble with another, since passing the evaluation would then cost $1,000 of real money on Hyperliquid in exchange for an account worth $480, leaving the two outcomes $1,120 apart, a wider spread than if the account had not been hedged at all.

4.4 Does Skill Still Matter Once Hedged?

Once the account is hedged at these ratios, the trader's skill stops affecting its value. Section 3.2 showed that normal trading is worth anywhere from −$41 to +$480 depending on skill, yet at the ratios in equation (6) the hedged account is worth $100 for every type of trader, because the hedge gains whenever the trader loses simulated money and loses whenever the trader makes it, so skill cancels out and what remains is simply the gap between the fee and the value of the contract. Figure 12 compares the two cases.

This matters most for exactly the traders Vest's pricing relies on, since for the break-even trader normal trading is worth nothing while the hedged account is still worth $100. A genuinely skilled trader, by contrast, gives up upside by hedging fully, so the size of the hedge becomes a choice between keeping the benefit of skill and removing risk, a trade-off that Figure 13 maps in full.

On real prices, the strategy sits in the range where the hedge is most useful, because the daily loss limit made the evaluation behave like a trader who passes about 28% of the time (Section 6.2), between the break-even trader and the trader with no skill, and this is why the hedged strategy outperforms normal trading in the simulations that follow.

4.6 Could the Hedge Be Liquidated?

Because the hedge loses money while the evaluation moves toward its target, its leverage has to leave room for that move, since a liquidation before the evaluation passed would leave the strategy losing on both sides at once. A Hyperliquid position at leverage \(L\) is liquidated after an adverse move of

\[ \begin{aligned} m(L) &= \frac{1/L - \mu}{1 + \mu}, \\ \mu &= \frac{1}{2\,L_{\max}}, \end{aligned} \tag{8} \]

where \(\mu\) is the maintenance margin rate and \(L_{\max} = 30\) is the maximum leverage on the contract. Requiring \(m(L) \ge 2\,G/N\) keeps liquidation at least twice as far away as the evaluation target, which for the running example permits leverage of up to 20.

5. How the Strategy Is Tested

The strategy is tested on real prices, with every fee charged and every rule fixed before the first test was run. The tests combine paired Vest and Hyperliquid prices that are checked against my own trades, a simulator that reproduces the closed-form value exactly before any real price is introduced, and a single trading rule applied unchanged to every account, so the results measure the contract itself and none of them measure a strategy tuned to fit the data.

5.2 Can the Prices Be Trusted?

The price data matches both my own trades and Vest's published bars, so the results rest on prices that were actually tradeable. Of 26 trades I executed on Vest, 24 fall inside the matching one-minute range and the other two fall in minutes with no recorded trades, while hourly bars rebuilt from the minute data match Vest's published hourly bars to within 0.3 basis points at the close over 520 hours, a match that disappears, with differences above 10 basis points, if the bars are shifted by a single hour. 5.4 How Is Each Account Simulated?

The simulator turns real prices into complete account lives, with each path covering the evaluation, the funded stage if it is reached, and the account's closure. Paths are built by reshuffling whole trading days of joint Vest and Hyperliquid hourly prices, a method known as a block bootstrap, which preserves both the pattern of volatility within each day and the way the two exchanges move together. The trading rule was fixed in advance and holds one directional position until a limit is reached, closing it for the day when the day's loss reaches 85% of the daily limit and reopening it at the next reset, staying flat over weekends and withdrawing funded profit at a fixed threshold, with each path run both long and short. On random prices without costs or a daily limit the simulator gives $100.00, exactly the value of equation (5), and Figure 17 sets out the process.

The baseline strategy follows the same rules in every simulation, including the holdout, and Figure 16 follows one account through them on real prices:

  • Position. A fixed $82,173 Nasdaq 100 position on Vest, identical for every account, in the evaluation and once funded. The size is set so that the $600 loss limit equals 0.75 of a typical day's move (equation (9)).
  • Direction. No forecast is made. Each account takes the opposite direction to the previous one, and every simulated path is run both long and short.
  • Hedge. An opposite Hyperliquid position of $18,489 during the evaluation and $49,304 once funded, opened and resized at the same moment as the Vest position.
  • Stops. A stop-market order closes the position for the day when the day's loss reaches 85% of Vest's 3% daily limit, and the position reopens at the 20:00 reset. Vest's −$600 limit ends the account.
  • Targets. The evaluation passes at +$1,000. Once funded, a payout is claimed whenever funded profit reaches $300.
  • Weekends and costs. Both legs close before Vest's Friday lock, and every measured fee, spread and slippage is charged.

Position size is expressed through \(b\), which measures the loss limit in units of the position's typical daily move:

\[ N = \frac{D}{b\,\sigma_{d}}, \tag{9} \]

where \(\sigma_d\) is the daily return volatility of the contract, 0.97% over the sample, so that the running example's choice of \(b = 0.75\) implies a position of \(N \approx \$82{,}000\).

5.6 How Is the Best Configuration Chosen?

For any investor below the ten-account cap, the binding constraint is the capital the strategy ties up, so configurations are chosen to maximise expected real profit per trading day for each dollar of deployed capital,

\[ \max_{\theta} \; \frac{\mathbb{E}[\Pi_\theta] \,/\, \mathbb{E}[T_\theta]}{K_\theta}, \tag{10} \]

where \(\theta\) collects the evaluation type, position size \(b\), hedge multiple, withdrawal threshold and hedge leverage, \(T_\theta\) is the account's life in trading days and \(K_\theta\) is the 95th percentile of deployed capital. Each candidate must also clear three conditions: mean profit above two standard errors, a probability of real loss within a specified limit, and the liquidation constraint of Section 4.6.

In plain terms, the objective selects the way of running the account that earns the most each day for every dollar of capital it requires, while keeping the share of losing accounts within a set limit.

The search narrows the options in three stages. A screen first runs all 57 evaluations at five position sizes under each execution case, 855 configurations with 400 account lives each; a refinement then takes the five most capital-efficient evaluations and varies position size, hedge size, withdrawal threshold and leverage, 320 configurations with 1,000 account lives each; and an integer program finally chooses how many of each configuration to hold for every capital budget and loss limit (Section 7.3). Figure 18 shows how the set narrows.

6.5 Which Stop Order Protects the Account?

The daily stop closes the position for the day as the day's loss approaches Vest's 3% daily limit, and its placement leaves very little margin for error: it sits 31 bp from entry, while Vest's hard limit, which closes the account permanently, lies only 5.5 bp beyond it. Because about a third of daily stops are triggered by wicks, brief spikes that reverse within minutes, I tested whether a different type of stop could avoid these false exits without endangering the account, comparing four rules:

  • Stop-market. When the price touches the stop, the position is sold immediately at the best available price.
  • Stop-limit. When the price touches the stop, a sell order is placed at the stop price, which never sells below that price but remains unfilled if the price continues to fall.
  • One-minute close. The position is sold only after a one-minute bar closes below the stop, so spikes that reverse within the minute are ignored.
  • Five-minute close. The same rule with five-minute bars.

The stop-market order is the only rule that protects the account. The three alternatives reduce false exits from about a third to about a quarter, but each of them waits before selling, and with the hard limit only 5.5 bp beyond the stop it is precisely that delay that allows the account to be breached: the stop-limit order remained unfilled as the price kept falling and reached the hard limit in 24% of cases, while the one-minute close let the price pass the limit 62% of the time and the five-minute close 82% of the time. Since a false stop costs one day of trading whereas a breach costs the whole account, the stop-market order is retained, and the cost of wicks is already included in every result as part of the daily-limit cost in Section 6.2.

6.6 Does the Time of Day Matter?

Trading activity varies a great deal through the day, which affects two decisions in particular, when to open an account and when to adjust the hedge. Figure 21 shows the average one-minute price move on Vest by hour of the New York day, and Figure 22 shows how much the price gap between Vest and Hyperliquid moves by hour, which matters because that gap is the one risk the hedge cannot remove.

The two measures peak at different times. Prices move most just after the 9:30 open, when the first fifteen minutes average 7.7 bp a minute, about three times the 2 to 2.5 bp of the overnight hours, and the 9:00 to 11:00 window carries a quarter of the day's volume, whereas the gap between the exchanges rises only slightly at the open and moves most at 17:00 New York time, when the Nasdaq futures market takes its daily break and trading on Vest almost stops. Four rules follow from this, each leaving expected profit unchanged while reducing the risk of a poor fill or an early stop:

  • Avoid opening accounts or resizing the hedge in the first hour after 9:30, when a new position is more likely to hit its daily stop on the first move.
  • Keep the hedge unchanged around 17:00 New York time, when the gap between exchanges is least stable.
  • Track Vest's daily reset at 20:00 New York time, when the daily loss limit restarts and the daily stop can be re-entered.
  • Plan around Friday: Vest reduces index leverage from 16:00 and locks the market from 17:00 until Sunday evening, so positions are closed before then.

Scheduled news calls for the same care as the open, because inflation and employment data at 8:30 and Federal Reserve decisions at 14:00 cause short spikes that hourly averages smooth over, although the effect of these rules on returns is still to be simulated.

6.7 Can Smarter Sizing Recover Some of the Cost?

Since the daily loss limit is the largest single cost (Section 6.2), the natural question is whether a smarter sizing rule could recover part of it, and theory sets a clear limit on what sizing can achieve. For an account whose profit has no trend, the optional stopping theorem implies that no sizing rule changes the probability of reaching the target before the loss limit, so a sizing rule can affect only three things: how quickly the account resolves, how much it costs to trade, and how often the daily stop forces an exit near an intraday low that later recovers.

I tested two rules against the fixed-size baseline. Under the risk-budget rule, which follows the logic of constant proportion portfolio insurance, the position shrinks in proportion to the room left before the daily stop, down to a floor of 25% or 50% of full size, and returns to full size at the daily reset. Under the time-of-day rule, the position is scaled by the hourly volatility profile, from 0.5 to 1.5 times full size, so that the dollar risk in each hour is roughly constant. In both rules the hedge is resized with the position, so the hedge ratio is unchanged, and both legs are rebalanced whenever the target moves by more than 10% of full size, paying planned-trade costs. Each rule was simulated on the same 5,000 account lives as the baseline, on the main sample and on the holdout, so every difference in Table 12 comes from the rule and none from the price paths.

7.3 How Many Accounts, and Which?

Combining configurations into a portfolio turns per-account results into a monthly return for a given budget. Since an investor can hold several evaluations at once, up to ten funded, for a capital budget \(B\) and a loss limit \(\bar{p}\) I choose integer holdings \(n_k\) of each eligible configuration \(k\) to solve

\[ \begin{aligned} \max_{\{n_k\}} \quad & \sum_{k} n_k\,\pi_k \\ \text{subject to} \quad & \sum_{k} n_k K_k \le B, \\ & \sum_{k} n_k \le 10, \quad n_k \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}, \end{aligned} \tag{11} \]

where \(\pi_k\) is expected real profit per trading day, \(K_k\) is deployed capital and eligible configurations satisfy \(P(\text{loss}) \le \bar{p}\). Each solution is then simulated over a 21-trading-day month, and Table 16 reports the result for each budget and loss limit.

8. Does It Hold Out of Sample?

The result holds in every out-of-sample test, including a holdout and a forward test on prices recorded after the strategy was fixed. It avoids the three mistakes behind most too-good-to-be-true trading results, a strategy fitted to past prices, prices that were never tradeable and costs that were left out, because the value comes from the contract terms, the prices match my own fills and every measured cost is charged. Since Vest is new and the sample is therefore short, the checks that follow are designed around exactly that weakness, running from a forward test that updates daily and the strategy run in calendar order to separate time periods, adverse scenarios, sensitivity to the main settings and formal tests for overfitting.

8.1 Has It Worked Since the Design Was Fixed?

The most direct evidence is the forward test at the top of this paper (Figure S1), which runs accounts back to back on prices recorded since 29 October 2026, the day after the strategy was fixed, and updates every day. Because its counts change as accounts close, they are reported there and nowhere else, and what matters for this section is the shape of the result, a hedged book that climbs in small steps while the same accounts without the hedge swing between lost fees and occasional large payouts.

8.6 Could the Result Be Overfitted?

Searching many settings will always find one that looks good, so the design limits how much was searched and then tests what was. Every study's expected result was recorded before it ran (Appendix D.3) and the trading rule has no fitted parameters, while the settings that were compared, 16 stop and target levels, 12 entry-rule tests and three sizing rules, were judged only after adjusting for the number of trials with the deflated Sharpe ratio, the probability of backtest overfitting and a false-discovery-rate correction (Appendix C.5). No stop, target or entry setting survived those adjustments, which is why the strategy uses none of them, and the one refinement that did survive, risk-budget sizing, was confirmed in the holdout before it entered the implementation plan.

9. How It Was Built

Each step of the analysis needed a tool that did not exist, so the tools were built as the work went. The minute prices, the research engine and the trading platform described below were not side projects, since each was built at the point where the analysis could not go further without it.

9.1 Where Did the Minute Data Come From?

Vest publishes hourly price history, but the stop-rule, timing and validation studies needed prices minute by minute, so the minute data comes from a recorder in my own trading software that has captured Vest's public trade feed since August 2026. Those prices are checked against my own executed trades (Section 5.2), and the same recorder now feeds the daily forward test at the top of this paper.

9.2 How Can the Numbers Be Checked?

All of the analysis runs in a Python research package of 19 pre-registered studies, built around a lifecycle simulator that reproduces the closed-form value to the cent on idealised prices before any real price is used, and backed by 44 automated tests covering the accounting, the account rules, the formulas and the fee schedules. Every number in this paper is read from a results file produced by that code, and the commands in Appendix E regenerate all of it.

9.3 How Is the Strategy Run?

A strategy is only worth something if it can be run, so the Vest side sits on a trading platform I built for Vest accounts, Vest Autopilot. The platform runs strategies written in Pine Script or NinjaScript or sent as TradingView alerts, and it sizes every trade from the account's own loss limits through a risk kernel with 67 acceptance tests, manages stops on every price tick, re-reads the account before every live order, records each order's intent before sending it and freezes if an outcome is uncertain, with a kill switch that pauses entries and flattens paper positions. It runs unattended as a supervised Windows service with a watchdog, a Chrome companion and webhooks routed through Tailscale, and its core service of about 14,600 lines of Python carries 311 application tests. The Hyperliquid hedge is the piece still to be wired in, which makes it the first step of the implementation plan (Section 10.5).

9.4 How Was It Built So Quickly?

Both systems were built largely by directing AI coding agents from written specifications, with every change required to pass the test suites before it was kept, 378 tests on the platform and 44 on the research engine. The platform began as a written brief of about 17,000 characters setting out the objective, the safety constraints and the open-source projects to study, and the research followed the same pattern of specifying, building, testing and checking. The judgement stayed human, in deciding what to build, what to test and what to trust, while the agents supplied the speed, a combination that this kind of work increasingly rewards.

9.5 What Is Being Built Now?

The part being built now is the one the results depend on most, namely sending the Vest order and the Hyperliquid hedge together as a single action. Every stress test points to the same weakness, since slow or slipping hedge execution is the only thing that turns the result negative (Section 8.4), so the next layer of the platform is a Python execution engine that fires both legs concurrently through each exchange's API, measures the gap between the two fills and treats the pair as one position from the moment it is sent.

Most of the work lies in the cases where something goes wrong, because that is where a hedged strategy actually loses money, and the engine is being designed around them:

  • One leg fills and the other does not, for example when a post-only order is cancelled because the price moved; the engine must complete the hedge at once or unwind the filled leg, within a fixed limit on unhedged exposure.
  • Partial fills, where the hedge is resized to the quantity actually filled on Vest.
  • Unknown outcomes, such as a timeout after an order is sent; the engine records each order's intent before sending it and freezes until both exchanges confirm what happened, the rule the Vest platform already follows.
  • Disconnection or an exchange outage, covered by native stops on each venue and by reconciling positions against both exchanges on reconnect.
  • Latency at the moments that matter, a pass, a daily stop or a close, where the hedge must follow Vest within seconds and every delay is measured against the 20 basis point slippage limit of Section 8.4.

Building this, with its concurrency, exchange APIs, idempotent order handling and failure recovery, is the most direct way to turn the paper's main open risk into a measured number, and the paper-hedge stage of the implementation plan (Section 10.5) is designed to measure exactly that.

10. Risks and Recommendations

The risks that remain are contractual and operational, and each can be tested cheaply before serious capital is committed. Whether Vest's terms permit the strategy, whether payouts arrive and whether the hedge executes as modelled are the open questions, and the section sets them out together with the limitations of the analysis, recommendations for an investor and for Vest, the next tests and a staged plan for implementation.

10.1 What Are the Open Risks?

The most important open risk is whether the strategy complies with Vest's terms. Vest's programme terms, updated on 20 October 2026, restrict misuse of multiple accounts and of price differences between venues and give Vest discretion over payouts, and although an offsetting position on another exchange is a hedge on the account, distinct from trading the price difference between the exchanges, and Section 6.2 shows that the gap contributes roughly nothing to profit, Vest still decides how its terms apply. Anyone acting on this analysis should therefore read the current terms and consult Vest if unsure, since this paper studies how the product is priced and is not a recommendation to trade it.

The paper assumes claims are paid, which matches Vest's documented process and my own funded accounts, and a refused payout would cost at most the capital deployed to that account, about $3,255 in the baseline strategy, so removing the edge would require a refusal rate far above anything I have observed. In live execution, three findings would change the conclusion, namely hedge orders slipping by more than about 20 basis points per trade, Vest limit orders rarely filling, or accounts passing far less often than the simulated 28%, and because each can be tested cheaply before committing meaningful capital, Section 10.4 lists them as the next steps.

10.2 What Are the Limitations?

The main limitation is that the hedge has not yet been traded live, so reaction time, partial fills and margin calls are modelled rather than observed, and the results are most sensitive to exactly these factors. The sample covers three months of hourly data on one main contract pair, the limit-order case assumes every resting order fills, Vest's bid–ask spread on index markets is inferred from eleven stop fills, and all results come from a single trading rule, while claims are assumed to be paid in full even though Vest retains discretion under its terms, which also restrict misuse of multiple accounts.

10.3 What Should Be Done?

For an investor, the case supports a small, staged commitment. The edge is real but capped near $24,000 a month, and its one material downside, slow or slipping hedge execution, can be measured with a single account and a paper hedge before any further capital is committed, using the baseline account, the risk-budget sizing rule and the timing rules of Section 6 as the starting configuration.

For Vest, the cleanest fix is price. A fee near $180 would remove the value for a mechanical buyer, and a trailing loss limit or a time limit during the evaluation would narrow it, while leaving the economics against the typical customer, who already passes well below the break-even rate, unchanged (Section 3.3).

10.4 What Comes Next?

The next steps focus on the hedge, because the results are most sensitive to it, beginning with a two-week paper test of the hedge against live Vest limit events to measure reaction time and slippage, followed by a recording of Vest's index order books to measure spreads and fill rates, and finally an extension of the sample into a different volatility regime.

10.5 How Should It Be Implemented?

A live implementation should proceed in stages, each with a test that must be passed before the next begins, because the main uncertainty is operational and not statistical:

  1. Automated execution on Vest. Orders, stops, the daily stop rule and reconciliation of every fee and payment handled by software, so that the fixed trading rule is followed exactly, including the risk-budget sizing rule of Section 6.7.
  2. Paper hedge. The Hyperliquid hedge run on paper against live Vest limit events for at least two weeks, to measure reaction time and slippage. The strategy fails if slippage approaches 20 bp per hedge trade (Section 8.4).
  3. Small live test. One account hedged with real money, with hard limits on exposure and loss that sit outside the strategy code, a kill switch, and reconciliation against the exchange before and after every order.
  4. Scaling. The optimal book of Section 7.3, sized by the rule in Section 7.4, built up within Vest's ten-account limit and its terms, with accounts opened at different times and in alternating directions so that they do not behave as one position.

11. Conclusion

Vest Capital's evaluations are worth more than they cost, and a hedge sized to the account's real exposure captures the difference without any forecasting skill. The $10,000 evaluation is worth $180 against an $80 fee because Vest prices for customers who trade worse than a coin flip, a choice that suits its business and leaves room for a buyer who trades mechanically, and about half of that value survives real prices and measured costs, giving roughly 11 percent a month on capital up to $150,000 and a ceiling near $24,000 a month.

What the strategy has not yet done is run with real money on both sides. The Vest side runs on a platform built for it and the hedge side is simulated, and the staged plan in Section 10.5 exists to close that gap at the lowest possible cost. Until then the result is a hedge fund in miniature: one mispriced opportunity, one risk-managed strategy, and a capacity limit set by the firm selling the product.

A.2 Pass Probability and Expected Duration

Assume no skill: \(X_t = \sigma W_t\), a driftless Brownian motion, with barriers at \(+G\) and \(-D\). Let \(\tau\) be the first time either is hit. \(X\) is a bounded martingale up to \(\tau\), so by optional stopping:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[X_\tau] &= 0 \\ G\,P - D\,(1 - P) &= 0 \\ P(\text{pass}) &= \frac{D}{G + D} \end{aligned} \]

Applying optional stopping to the martingale \(X_t^2 - \sigma^2 t\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[X_\tau^2] &= \sigma^2\, \mathbb{E}[\tau] \\ G^2 P + D^2 (1 - P) &= \sigma^2\, \mathbb{E}[\tau] \\ \mathbb{E}[\tau] &= \frac{G \cdot D}{\sigma^2} \end{aligned} \]

\(N\) does not appear in \(P(\text{pass})\). It enters \(\mathbb{E}[\tau]\) through \(\sigma \propto N\): doubling the position quarters the expected time.

A.3 Funded Account Value

With profit withdrawn as it arises, the balance stays at or below its start, so a funded account closes when cumulative profit falls \(D\) below its running maximum. For a driftless process the running maximum at that moment has mean \(D\) (Lehoczky, 1977). A trader who claims profit as it arises therefore receives on average

\[ V_{\text{funded}} = s \cdot D \]

A.4 Multi-Step Evaluations

\[ \begin{aligned} P(\text{pass all}) &= \prod_{k=1}^{K} \frac{D}{G_k + D} = \frac{1}{A}, \\ A &= \prod_{k=1}^{K}\left(1 + \frac{G_k}{D}\right), \\ V &= \frac{s \cdot D}{A} - F \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} \text{1-Step:}\quad A &= 1 + \tfrac{1{,}000}{600} = 2.667, \quad V = \$100 \\ \text{2-Step:}\quad A &= 4.89, \quad P(\text{pass all}) = 20.5\% \end{aligned} \]

A.5 Hedge Ratio

Let \(V_{k+1}\) be the value of reaching the next stage. With hedge \(h_k\) on step \(k\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Fail:}\quad & h_k D - F \\ \text{Pass:}\quad & V_{k+1} - h_k G_k - F \end{aligned} \]

Setting them equal:

\[ h_k = \frac{V_{k+1}}{D + G_k}, \qquad h_{\text{funded}} = s \]

This is the change in real value per simulated dollar, the account's real-money delta. It removes the dependence of the real result on whether the step passes. On driftless prices the hedge has zero expected profit, so the expected value is unchanged. For the worked example \(h = 480/1{,}600 = 0.30\); for two-step accounts \(h = 0.16\) on step one and \(0.44\) on step two.

A.6 Discrete Monitoring and Liquidation

Following Broadie, Glasserman and Kou (1997), barriers monitored at interval \(\Delta t\) are equivalent to continuously monitored barriers shifted outward by

\[ \beta \, \sigma \sqrt{\Delta t}, \qquad \beta \approx 0.5826 \]

With hourly checks this raises the pass rate from 37.5% to 38.2%, matching the hourly simulation (37.9%). A Hyperliquid position at leverage \(L\) is liquidated after an adverse move of

\[ m(L) = \frac{1/L - \mu}{1 + \mu}, \qquad \mu = \frac{1}{2 L_{\max}} \]

The search requires \(m(L) \ge 2\,G/N\).

A.7 Trader Skill

Let simulated profit have drift \(\mu\), and measure skill by \(\kappa = 2\mu D/\sigma^{2}\), with \(\kappa = 0\) for no skill. The probability of passing a step with target \(G\) is

\[ P_\kappa = \frac{1 - e^{-\kappa}}{1 - e^{-\kappa (1 + G/D)}}, \]

and the expected profit claimed from a funded account before it closes is \(D\,(e^{\kappa}-1)/\kappa\). Normal trading is therefore worth

\[ V_{\text{normal}}(\kappa) = P_\kappa \, s\,D\,\frac{e^{\kappa}-1}{\kappa} - F. \]

With the hedge at \(h = sD/(G+D)\) before funding and \(s\) after, the hedge loses exactly what skill adds, and the value is the same for every \(\kappa\):

\[ V_{\text{hedged}} = h\,D - F = 0.30 \times 600 - 80 = \$100. \]

The break-even trader solves \(V_{\text{normal}}(\kappa) = 0\): \(\kappa = -0.56\), a pass probability of 21.8%.

\[ N = \frac{D}{b \cdot \sigma_{\text{daily}}}, \qquad \sigma_{\text{daily}} \approx 0.97\%\ \text{of price} \]

DecisionAccepted. Keep stop-market orders; apply timing rules (Section 6.6).
LimitationSix weeks of minute data; moves within a minute are not visible.

Study 18. Performance in time order

QuestionDoes the strategy hold up when accounts run back to back on the real price history, without resampling?
MethodEight account slots, each starting on a different day, running the baseline strategy account after account on the hourly history from 30 July to 28 October 2026.
FindingEvery hedged slot made money ($792 to $1,175 over about 19 accounts) with a worst drawdown of $47. The same accounts unhedged ended between −$1,365 and +$984.
DecisionAccepted. Section 8.2.
LimitationOne three-month history; the eight slots overlap in time.

Study 19. Position sizing within the day

QuestionCan sizing the position against the room left before the daily stop, or by time of day, recover part of the daily-limit cost?
MethodRisk-budget sizing with 25% and 50% floors and time-of-day volatility scaling, each simulated on the same 5,000 account lives as the fixed-size baseline, on the main sample and the holdout; paired differences.
FindingThe 25% risk budget adds $3.61 ± $0.86 per account on the main sample and $11.82 ± $0.95 in the holdout, and lowers losing accounts from 10.2% to 7.7%. The 50% floor is mixed, and time-of-day scaling loses $13.75 per account.
DecisionPartly accepted. Fixed sizing is kept for the headline results, and risk-budget sizing is added to the implementation plan (Section 6.7).
LimitationHourly steps only; two floors tested; found after the main study was designed.

D.2 Alternatives Considered

  • Statistical hedge ratios. About 1.0; they hedge the price, not the account's real value.
  • Rebalancing the hedge mid-stage. A hedge left for three days drifts 4 bp from target; rebalancing adds fees for nothing.
  • Volatility-scaled sizing. Short look-backs over-correct: risk fell to 63% of its calm level in volatile periods.
  • Dynamic and learned policies. An exact dynamic programme shows that with costs and no skill the best policy is a few large bets, which the fixed rule already follows.
  • The author's own trading signals. Excluded: confidential and fewer than ten live signals.
